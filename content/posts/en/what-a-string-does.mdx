Press a fret and you shorten a string; turn a peg and you stretch it. Both change the same number - the pitch - and the reason they do is one tidy equation that has been hiding in plain sight on every guitar I have ever owned.

## The formula

The fundamental frequency of a vibrating string depends on just three things: its length $L$, its tension $T$, and its mass per unit length $\mu$[^mu].

$$
f = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}
$$

Read it slowly and the instrument makes sense. Halve the length (the twelfth fret) and the frequency doubles - an octave up. Raise the tension and the note rises too, but only with the square root, which is why a peg turn at the top feels so touchy.

<Callout kind="info">
The bass strings aren't just *looser* - they're heavier. A bigger $\mu$ lets a low string stay at a playable tension instead of flapping. That's what the winding is for.
</Callout>

## Standard tuning

Six open strings, low to high - the starting point every other shape is measured against:

Tuning, then, is never a single right answer: temper one interval and you bend another. The string doesn't care about our names for the notes - it just solves that equation, over and over, sixty times a second.

[^mu]: $\mu$ (mu) is the *linear mass density* - mass per unit length, in kg/m. It's why a thick wound string and a thin plain one can sit at the same tension yet sound an octave apart.
