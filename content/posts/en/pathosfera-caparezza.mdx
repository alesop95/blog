It's a masterful song (like the whole album), and it is so on many levels. It's interesting
already harmonically and in terms of sound, because the electronics are so delicate under a
ballad like this that they never let a lyric this staggering disappear. It's all the 80s
synthwave electronics Caparezza uses across the whole album, and here it sits there a little
delicate, like "fake strings", with a tiny solo. All of it over a metre of the lyrics that,
in my opinion, is itself carefully crafted.

Then, well, the lyrics. The main theme is staggering, and so is the way he – I think it's
autobiographical – manages to describe what he's saying. While doing all this, he manages to
quote Aldo, Giovanni e Giacomo and Pavlov in the same lyric. There's a part where I recognise
myself too, and maybe that's the one that moved me a little.

In general it took me back to that mood and those feelings in which music – back when I was
studying – let me surrender to it in a very particular way. These will sound like grand
considerations for this specific track, but music as an art form is beautiful for this too:
everyone has the freedom, and the beauty, of recognising themselves in specific aspects –
beyond grasping the objective shades of quality, the ones that make me lose myself in a world
entirely my own, that alienates me in a positive sense. The song is about withdrawing from
emotional suffering through rationality used as anaesthesia, which inevitably leads to a
detachment from the world: the emotional void, the loss of empathy. But in the final part
there's the return to vulnerability and empathy, because there's the need to recover emotions
after such a protection. And here I identify a lot, I admit it.

"Pathosfera" is a neologism Caparezza coined: it's that dimension, that fictional place where
emotions and intense urges really live – the ones we flee from, causing closure in the
process. In the end emotional anaesthesia (truly impossible for us humans) is the thing that
switches off the light, that stops you feeling joy. When he returns to pathos (hence the name
of this dimension) there's the return of emotions, but also of vulnerability, and with it the
move back toward the human world and its complexity.

This intrinsic adversative is, in truth, inescapable – but in that inescapability lies, in my
view, the beauty of what distinguishes a human being from a beast. And it's fascinating: read
this way, through empathy toward such an inner journey, it takes on a meaning and an angle
that are anything but negative.
